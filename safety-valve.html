Overall layout and mechanical assembly

The assembly combines a control console and a fixture-side test area. I modeled the structural frames, outer panels, service doors, fixture interfaces, and sliding enclosure as parts of the complete system. The exploded view shows how those parts fit together.

Layout versus access

The equipment had to fit inside the enclosure while leaving access to instruments, piping, fasteners, and the valve-loading area.

Complete assembly versus isolated parts

I modeled the rail mounts, panels, frames, and fixture together, then addressed rail–frame interference during physical assembly.

Fixture concepts and contact geometry

The fixture needed to accommodate DN15–DN100 valve flanges. I compared clamping concepts using gripping requirements, FEA, and material cost. The selected architecture uses three eccentric elements for radial positioning and hydraulic axial loading; a timing belt coordinates the eccentric elements.

The important tradeoff was contact geometry as well as overall strength: a jaw can carry load while still concentrating that load at its contact with the flange. That interface became the focus of the FEA review.

FEA setup and geometry refinement

I used SolidWorks Simulation to examine the fixture load path and the jaw–flange interface. A one-third symmetric model reduced the solve size while preserving the repeated clamping geometry.

Symmetry and simplification

I modeled one 120° sector: one eccentric jaw, its load-transfer components, and the matching flange segment. Symmetry fixtures on the cut faces constrained circumferential motion. Small non-load-bearing features were suppressed to simplify meshing.

Jaw–flange contact

I defined local no-penetration contact between the jaw underside and flange surface with a friction coefficient of 0.15. Linear-elastic material models supported the static stress review.

Mesh control

I used curvature-based, second-order tetrahedral elements and refined the jaw–flange contact region to a 1 mm local element size. Local refinement concentrated resolution where the stress gradient was largest.

Solution and review

I enabled large-displacement behavior in the static study and used the Direct Sparse solver. I reviewed von Mises stress, the local contact region, and displacement to guide the contact-edge revision.

FEA showed localized stress concentration at the jaw–flange interface. I increased the transition radius at the contact edge to R10 mm to address it.

Rectangular-tube frames and sheet metal

I modeled the welded 80 × 40 × 4 mm rectangular-tube frames, then developed the 2 mm sheet-metal enclosure around them. The panels include bends, instrument cutouts, access openings, rear doors, and reinforcement.

Fastening and reinforcement details

Small-countersunk, half-hex rivet nuts provide a nearly flush interface for panels and hinges while resisting rotation during tightening. Rear-door reinforcement supports the large sheet-metal panel; welded M6 × 18 studs mount the door-lock hardware from the inside. At the pump mounts, Ø18 × 30 mm inserts with M10 threads carry fastening loads through solid supports inside the hollow tube, with rubber isolators at the pump feet.

Panel stiffness versus simplicity

Reinforcement supports the large rear door while keeping the outer face as a sheet-metal panel.

Mounting strength versus hollow-tube geometry

Internal supports provide a fastening load path at selected tube-frame mounting locations.

Sliding enclosure and rail interfaces

I modeled the sliding-door frame, its rail mounting plates, the sheet-metal window surround, and the retaining hardware. These interfaces connect a moving enclosure to the stationary fixture-side frame.

Two 5 mm polycarbonate window panels are held by retaining strips and M6 × 20 self-clinching fasteners around the sheet-metal surround. Four welded mounting plates provide the sliding frame’s rail interfaces. Dedicated U-section sheet-metal brackets separate the stationary rail installation from the side cover.

Fabrication and on-site troubleshooting

CAD fit and manufacturing clearance

The nominal panel-to-frame fit had zero clearance. During manufacturing, weld distortion, bend springback, and bend-radius interference required 0.5–1.5 mm seam allowances. The manufacturing team adjusted the flat patterns and finished the seams by welding and grinding; my on-site troubleshooting focused on the rail–frame interface.

I prepared the mechanical BOM and fabrication-ready STEP files for vendors and worked on the physical assembly. The build connected the modeled sheet-metal panels, welded frames, rail interfaces, and fastening details to the manufactured parts.
